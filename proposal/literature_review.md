# Literature Review: U.S. Student Policy and Online Discourse

> Draft for the project proposal. Scope: policy developments during the 2025–2026 Trump administration and online discussions concerning international students, with an India-linked subset where identification is defensible. Studies of the first Trump administration are historical or methodological precedents, not evidence about current events.

## 1. Research problem and scope

Recent U.S. policy changes may affect international students both through formal rules and through uncertainty about whether studying in the United States remains feasible or desirable. Enrollment and visa figures can describe changes in student mobility, but they cannot show *which concerns students voiced as events unfolded*. This project therefore examines discussions surrounding specific policy announcements during the 2025–2026 Trump administration, with particular attention to Indian students where they can be identified reliably. It asks how the subjects of discussion and **expressed study plans** change across announcement periods. Visa issuance and enrollment data provide subsequent context, rather than a direct measure of what online participants decided.

The literature supplies parts of this design, but the parts need to be combined carefully. Student-mobility research examines policy and enrollment; online-community research examines students' questions; and computational communication research tracks discourse around political events. The proposed study connects these strands at the level of a **dated policy event and its surrounding discussion**.

## 2. Policy uncertainty and international-student mobility

[Omotilewa and Johnson (2025)](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/jcihe/vol17/iss4/8/) studied international graduate students who began their U.S. degrees in 2017–2020. Their mixed-method survey found negative perceptions of first-term Trump policies and rhetoric, with students reporting particular difficulty around visa status, travel bans, and OPT/CPT work authorization. The study also examines effects on students' well-being. It supports treating *visa process*, *travel*, and *work authorization* as distinct concerns in our coding scheme, rather than collapsing them into one "immigration sentiment" label. Its participants were already studying in the United States, however, so their accounts cannot establish how prospective Indian students in 2025–2026 responded to new announcements.

[Sharpe (2026)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/01979183261474498) provides a complementary view of observable mobility. Using country-year F-1 visa issuance and active-enrollment data, with fixed-effects and event-study analyses, Sharpe finds that countries targeted during the 2016 campaign experienced a marked post-election reversal in **new visa inflows**, while active enrollment changed less sharply. That difference matters for this project: a prospective student can reconsider applying well before the change becomes visible in the total number of students on campus. Sharpe's study motivates looking at stated intentions and later visa indicators as **different measures with different time scales**. It does not establish that online discussion caused the visa changes, and it concerns the 2016 election rather than the current administration.

The comparative study [*Examining the impacts of visa policy changes on international student enrolment and mobility* (2026)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075079.2026.2673034) places visa-policy changes alongside enrollment trends across the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia from 2017/18 to 2024/25. It provides a rationale for including *alternative destinations* in the project. Its broad country-level comparisons cannot tell us which individual policy prompted a particular student's decision. For this project, its value is to frame "considering Canada" or "deferring a U.S. offer" as a specific, codable response, while avoiding the assumption that every mention of another country represents a final decision.

**Implication for this study:** policy events should be recorded individually, with their announcement date, implementation date, affected visa categories, and legal status. A proposal, final rule, temporary pause, and effective change may generate different discussions. For example, the [August 2025 proposed duration-of-status rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/08/28/2025-16554/establishing-a-fixed-time-period-of-admission-and-an-extension-of-stay-procedure-for-nonimmigrant) and the [July 2026 final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/17/2026-14439/establishing-a-fixed-time-period-of-admission-and-an-extension-of-stay-procedure-for-nonimmigrant) should be separate timeline entries. Scholarly articles help interpret the issues; primary policy records establish what was announced and when. [NAFSA's 2025 policy digest](https://www.nafsa.org/policy-digest-2025) can be used as a secondary chronology, with each selected event checked against the underlying government source.

## 3. What student discussions can reveal

[Youm and colleagues (2026)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0341314) offer the closest precedent for the proposed Reddit analysis. They collected **8,006 posts and 33,981 comments** from r/f1visa between August 2015 and December 2022 using Pushshift. They applied BERTopic to identify recurring subjects and compared information-seeking before and during the COVID-19 period. Their findings show a shift from employment-related discussion toward travel, entry, and financial concerns during the pandemic. This demonstrates that a visa-focused community can reveal changing *information needs* when circumstances change. It also gives this project a practical model for retaining post dates, titles, bodies, and discussion context.

Their study also defines a boundary. A topic such as "visa interviews" identifies **what a post is about**, not whether its author plans to continue, defer, or abandon U.S. study. This project therefore needs a separate, manually validated **expressed-plan code**. Youm and colleagues also acknowledge that a single subreddit cannot represent all international students and that user characteristics are often unknown. An India-linked subset should be reported only when a post or its discussion context explicitly establishes that connection; an English-language post in r/f1visa cannot be assumed to come from an Indian student.

[Razavisousan and Joshi (2022)](https://ebiquity.umbc.edu/_file_directory_/papers/1158.pdf) show another way to build a student-mobility corpus. They developed search terms with input from international students and university international-office staff, then collected tweets containing student-mobility terms. Their resulting dataset included **more than seven million relevant tweets** and covered questions about visas, travel, OPT, CPT, employment, and remaining in the United States. The most useful lesson is their **domain-informed query construction**: search terms should be derived from the language students actually use, then checked for irrelevant matches. Their analysis identifies relationships among mobility factors; it does not classify individual students' responses to particular 2025–2026 announcements.

**Implication for this study:** the discussion dataset needs at least three separate labels: **speaker or source type** (student, adviser, journalist, organization, or unclear), **issue** (for example, interview delay or OPT), and **expressed plan** (continue, delay, withdraw, consider another destination, or no stated plan). A question, a rumor repeated by another user, and a first-person decision should not be counted as equivalent student intentions.

## 4. Why the announcement date matters

[Liu and Harlow (2020)](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/16571/3288/50218) collected **8,247 tweets from 40 immigrant-serving organizations** in the two months before and after the January 27, 2017 travel-ban order. They used topic modeling and quantitative comparisons to examine how organizations' frames, hashtags, and shared messages changed. Their contribution to this project is the logic of comparing discourse on either side of a **verified policy event**, while retaining who produced the text. Their speakers were advocacy organizations, however, so the results describe organizational framing rather than students' personal study decisions.

[Koylu and colleagues (2018)](https://www.geo-social.com/docs/Koylu_et_al_2018_AutoCarto2018_final.pdf) similarly analyzed immigration tweets from a month before and after the 2017 travel ban, examining changes in volume, topic, sentiment, and location. The study illustrates how a major announcement can produce a visible spike in attention. For this project, that spike is a reason to compare **the composition of discussion**, not merely the number of posts. A rise in visa-related messages could reflect news sharing or political argument rather than a rise in student visa problems. The paper is a preliminary conference study of broad immigration discourse, so its scale and results should not be presented as findings about international students.

**Implication for this study:** each event should have a documented collection window covering a baseline period, the announcement week, and a later period. Windows may need adjustment when announcements overlap. Results should show both post counts and the **proportion of relevant posts** assigned to each concern or plan, separated by source type.

## 5. News framing and public discussion are different evidence

[Fabregat and Severino (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1080/1041794X.2025.2523000) analyzed **30,064 online news articles from 13 U.S. sources** published between 2017 and 2020 using topic modeling and network analysis. They found that immigration framing varied with outlets' political orientation, while some coverage was more nuanced. Their work supports analyzing news as its **own corpus**: a news article can help identify when an event gained attention and how it was framed, but it cannot stand in for a student's reaction. The subject is broad first-term immigration coverage, so its identified frames should be tested against student-policy articles before adoption.

For the current administration, [Nukunu and colleagues (2026)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590051X26000444) analyze **1,483 YouTube comments** about 2025 Trump immigration and trade policies using sentiment analysis, topic modeling, and network analysis. This is useful evidence that video comments can be collected and analyzed as a distinct public-discussion source. Its commenters discuss labor and immigration broadly, however; their sentiments cannot be attributed to international students. In this project, YouTube comments beneath student-visa videos would still require screening for relevance and speaker identity before inclusion in a student-focused result. The authors provide a [dataset and notebook](https://zenodo.org/records/21159101), which can be inspected for methodological detail.

## 6. Synthesis and contribution

Taken together, these studies justify four parts of the proposed design. Student-mobility research identifies policy areas and shows why new visa issuance, current enrollment, and stated plans must be distinguished. The r/f1visa and mobility-tweet studies show how to collect and organize student-related discussion. Travel-ban studies show how to analyze changes around a dated announcement. News-framing research explains why media narratives must be examined separately from student accounts.

The contribution is the **combination**: a verified 2025–2026 student-policy timeline linked to relevant news and online discussions, with separate measures of **issue**, **speaker type**, and **explicitly stated study plan**. The project can then examine whether those measures change around announcements and whether later aggregate visa or enrollment indicators move in a compatible direction. It should describe these as observed associations. The available design does not, by itself, establish that a policy *caused* an individual decision or an enrollment change.

Two limitations deserve attention from the start. First, forum participants are a self-selected group, and nationality is often unknown; the Indian subset must be defined by transparent evidence, with uncertain cases excluded or labeled uncertain. Second, visa-related social-media screening may influence **whether and what students are willing to post**. A decline in visible criticism after a screening announcement would therefore be ambiguous: it could reflect changed views, changed participation, or self-censorship. These limits should shape the interpretation of the results, not be added only at the end.
